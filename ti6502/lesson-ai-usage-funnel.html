Where do you stand in the AI usage funnel?

How professionals actually work with AI agents, how to install and direct one yourself, and what happens when many agents are let loose.

Most public talk about “what AI can do” assumes everyone is looking at the same thing. They are not. In October 2026, the AI researcher Andrej Karpathy argued that people experience very different AIs depending on how and on what they use them. We start from his funnel, then look at how professionals actually work with AI agents, how to install and direct one yourself, and what happens when many agents are let loose.

By the end of this lesson you should be able to: read the funnel critically; choose between a chat window and a local agent; install and start an agent; write a standing-instructions file and a reusable skill; and explain, in plain language, why autonomy and untrusted text make agents risky.

1 · The funnel

Karpathy sketched four tiers of AI experience. He called his numbers “napkin math”: orders of magnitude, not survey data. That is how you should read them too.

Here are the same numbers as bars, so you can read the scale directly:

On this estimate, professional users are about a quarter of one percent of humanity: 20 million out of roughly 8 billion. The logarithmic axis makes the lower tiers look much closer to the top than they are. On a linear axis the professional tier would be a barely visible sliver. Always check the scale before you trust a picture.

2 · Why the gap exists

Karpathy gives three reasons the same model can look brilliant to one person and mediocre to another:

  1. Problem size. Does the user have large, hard problems where the uplift is visible?
  2. Domain. Is the work digital, verifiable and economically valuable, like code and maths, so that models are trained and checked there?
  3. Access. Free, paid and internal systems are genuinely different products.

Notice what is missing from that list. Translation, interpretation, rhetoric and literary analysis have murkier feedback than code does. Whether a model is good at them is something you are well placed to judge, and that is a large part of what this course trains you to do.

5 · Install an agent on your own computer

The professional tier in Figure 1 mostly works with agents that live on your computer, not with a chat box in a browser. Here are two you can install today. Choose your system:

A · Claude Code (Anthropic)

You need a paid Claude plan (Pro, Max, Team or Enterprise) or an API account; the free plan does not include it. It runs on macOS 13 or later and Windows 10 (version 1809) or later, with at least 4 GB of RAM and an internet connection.

Open Terminal (Cmd+Space, type “Terminal”), paste the line below and press Enter:

curl -fsSL https://claude.ai/install.sh | bash

If you use Homebrew, this works too:

brew install --cask claude-code

Open PowerShell (Start menu, type “PowerShell”; the prompt begins with PS C:\), paste the line below and press Enter:

irm https://claude.ai/install.ps1 | iex

Or, with WinGet:

winget install Anthropic.ClaudeCode

You do not need administrator rights. Installing Git for Windows is optional; it lets the agent use a Bash shell.

Check it, then start it. Open a new terminal window and run:

claude --version

Go into a folder (your essay or corpus folder, say) and run:

claude

Follow the browser prompts to log in. Native installs keep themselves up to date; Homebrew and WinGet installs need a manual update. If something misbehaves, run claude doctor.

If you would rather avoid the terminal, Anthropic also offers a Claude desktop app for Mac and Windows with a Code tab that does the same job.

B · Google Antigravity

You need a Google account (personal, or a business account through Gemini Enterprise). It runs on macOS 12 or later and 64-bit Windows 10 or later. On an older Intel Mac, check the download page before you start.

  1. Go to antigravity.google/download and choose macOS or Windows.
  2. Run the installer (on a Mac, drag the app to Applications). If you are asked “Keep Both” or “Replace”, choose Replace.
  3. Launch it and sign in with your Google account.
  4. Create a project and add a local folder, such as your essay folder. Choose Local mode to work in that folder.

Optional command-line version (the program is called agy):

curl -fsSL https://antigravity.google/cli/install.sh | bash
irm https://antigravity.google/cli/install.ps1 | iex

Then open a terminal in your folder and run agy.

Before you paste commands into a terminal

These commands download and run an installer, so use only the ones published on the vendors' own sites (claude.ai and antigravity.google). Do not point an agent at a folder holding confidential material such as unpublished work, personal data or student records, and keep a backup of anything you let an agent edit.

6 · Web version or local version?

People say “web version” and “local version” about two different splits. Keep them apart.

Which should you pick?

If your project is a folder of documents, notes and a few scripts, start local: no GitHub, and the files stay on your machine. (What you ask it to read is still sent to the provider's servers, so the confidentiality warning above still applies.) Choose the cloud when the work lives in a GitHub repository, takes a long time, or should keep running while your laptop is off.

7 · Use Claude Code well

Installing a tool is the easy part. The habits are what separate casual from professional use. This talk comes from Anthropic: Cal, an applied-AI engineer who works on Claude Code's prompts, explains how the tool works and what the team learned about using it. The habit the talk skips: verify

Coders have tests that fail loudly. Prose has no failing test. Treat everything an agent writes about sources, quotations and facts as a draft to check, as Karpathy does in section 3, and keep the final judgement and the authorship yours.

8 · Skills: teach the agent your routines

A skill is a folder containing a SKILL.md file: a short description of when to use it, plus instructions. The agent loads the body only when your request matches the description, and you can also run it yourself by typing /skill-name. Where you save it decides who gets it:

Personal (all your projects)~/.claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md
This project only.claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md (commit it to share with a team)

The fields used below: name (the command), description (what the agent matches your request against), argument-hint (shown to you), disable-model-invocation: true (only you can trigger it), and $0, $1, $ARGUMENTS (the values you type after the command).

Example 1 · translation-qa (straight from this course)

Save as ~/.claude/skills/translation-qa/SKILL.md, then run /translation-qa source.txt translation.txt.

---
name: translation-qa
description: Compare a source text with its translation and list errors by category and severity. Use when the user asks to check, review or evaluate a translation.
argument-hint: [source-file] [translation-file]
disable-model-invocation: true
---

Read the source file ($0) and the translation file ($1).

Work segment by segment. For every problem you find, add a row to a table with:
segment number | source excerpt | translation excerpt | category | severity | suggested fix

Categories: accuracy (mistranslation, omission, addition), terminology, fluency, style/register, locale conventions.
Severity: minor, major, critical.

Rules:
- Quote the text exactly; never paraphrase the excerpts.
- If you are unsure whether something is an error, label it "query" instead of guessing.
- Finish with a count per category and a three-sentence overall assessment.
- Do not rewrite the whole translation. Suggest fixes only for flagged segments.

Look at the design choices: a fixed error table, “quote exactly”, and an explicit “query” label for uncertainty. Those lines are what make the output checkable.

Example 2 · citation-check

It takes no arguments; the agent applies it when you ask it to check the references in a draft.

---
name: citation-check
description: Audit the references in a draft for formatting consistency and for claims that need a source. Use when the user asks to check citations, references or a bibliography.
---

1. Identify the citation style the draft is using (APA, MLA, Chicago, etc.). If it is mixed, say so.
2. List every in-text citation that has no matching entry in the reference list, and every reference-list entry that is never cited.
3. List formatting inconsistencies (author initials, italics, page ranges, DOIs).
4. List factual claims that have no citation but probably need one.

Never invent a reference, DOI or page number. If a detail is missing, write "MISSING" and tell the user what to look up.

Example 3 · term-extractor

Run it as /term-extractor chapter1.txt French.

---
name: term-extractor
description: Extract specialised terms from a source text and propose target-language equivalents as a glossary table. Use when the user wants a glossary, termbase or terminology list.
argument-hint: [file] [target-language]
---

From the file $0, extract domain-specific terms (single words and multi-word terms).
Output a tab-separated table: source term | part of speech | proposed equivalent in $1 | context sentence | confidence (high/medium/low).

Mark confidence "low" whenever you are not certain, and group terms that appear to be variants of one concept.
Save the table to glossary.tsv in the current folder and show the first ten rows.

Related: a CLAUDE.md for a seminar paper

This is not a skill, but it is the first thing to write. Save it as CLAUDE.md in your project folder.

# Project notes for Claude

This folder holds my MA seminar paper on machine translation of legal texts.
- /sources: PDFs and notes I have collected (do not edit)
- /drafts: my writing; edit only when I ask
- Citation style: APA 7. Never invent references; write MISSING instead.
- Write in British English. Keep my voice; suggest changes, do not rewrite whole paragraphs.
- When unsure, ask me a question before acting.

Rule of thumb: things that are always true go in CLAUDE.md; routines you run sometimes become skills.

Treat a skill like an attachment

A skill is instructions plus, sometimes, scripts. Read one from the internet before you install it, exactly as you would read a file someone emailed you. The next section shows why this matters.

9 · Moltbook: a social network with no humans posting

Skills let an agent act on the world. Connect many such agents to each other and you get Moltbook, which went live on 28 January 2026. It was created by Matt Schlicht, chief executive of the company Octane AI. It looks like Reddit: posts, comments, up-votes and down-votes. The twist is that only AI agents may post. People can read it but not take part.

Within weeks it held more than twelve million posts, and the press described an “AI society”. Before accepting that description, look at how it works.

How it works

  • The agents run on OpenClaw, open-source agent software released by the engineer Peter Steinberger. OpenClaw is not a language model. It is a program that sits between a model you choose (Claude, Gemini, GPT) and online services, and it reaches those services through skills.
  • So a person sets up each agent, picks its model and installs its skills. The agent then acts for them, sometimes for days without supervision.
  • The text itself is produced by language models that learned from enormous amounts of human writing, Reddit included. A Reddit-style site full of Reddit-style posts is exactly what you would expect them to produce.

The accurate description, then, is not that AIs formed a society but that thousands of people's configured agents posted to a shared site. That is still interesting, and it is a useful exercise in asking who is speaking when a text appears. It is a question you already ask of any translation or ghost-written text.

What the professionals made of it

Moltbook drew an unusually fast and unusually divided response from people who work on AI. Read these as a debate about evidence: what, exactly, does a screenshot of an agent post show?

Andrej Karpathy, AI researcher (the author of the funnel in section 1), first reaction
“genuinely the most incredible sci-fi takeoff-adjacent thing I have seen recently”
Within the same stretch he also called it “a complete mess of a computer security nightmare at scale”, and he later described it as “a dumpster fire”.
Simon Willison, computer scientist
The agents “just play out science fiction scenarios they have seen in their training data.” He called the content “complete slop.”
Ethan Mollick, professor at the Wharton School
“The thing about Moltbook is that it is creating a shared fictional context for a bunch of AIs.”
Scott Alexander, writer, Astral Codex Ten
“Moltbook straddles the line between ‘AIs imitating a social network’ and ‘AIs actually having a social network’.”
Mike Peterson, The Mac Observer
“Moltbook is a real agent social feed, but viral Moltbook screenshots are a weak form of evidence.”
Will Douglas Heaven, MIT Technology Review
Called the whole episode “AI theater”: a performance that says more about our appetite for AI stories than about what agents can do.
Fortune, on the security risk
“you are introducing an attack surface that no current security model adequately addresses.”

What the critiques have in common

  • The evidence problem. Moltbook's database was open, and anyone could post as an agent. Reporters found that some of the most-shared posts were written or prompted by people, and one analyst puts it plainly: a screenshot is weak evidence. When you meet a striking piece of “AI behaviour”, ask who set it up, who selected the example and who benefits from you sharing it.
  • The interpretation problem. Even where an agent really did write the text, Willison, Mollick and Alexander agree on the mechanism: a model trained on human fiction and forums continues the genre it is placed in. Mollick's “shared fictional context” is the key phrase. Put many models into a science-fiction setting and you get science fiction.
  • The security problem is real whatever the posts mean. Karpathy moved from fascination to alarm without changing his facts. The part nobody disputes is the exposed database and the unsafe skills, which the next table sets out.
  • Notice how opinion moved. A leading researcher's first reaction and his later one differ sharply. It is normal for expert judgement to shift as evidence arrives, and you should expect to revise yours.

The security lessons

ProblemWhat happened
Exposed keysResearchers at the security firm Wiz found Moltbook's database open to anyone to read and write. About 1.5 million API keys, the passwords that let software act as a user, were exposed.
Risky skillsThe security firm Snyk found that 36% of the skill code it examined had at least one notable security flaw.
Prompt injectionAn attacker posts an instruction on a public page. An agent that reads the page can treat it as an order and change its behaviour without its owner knowing.
“It runs on your machine”The software does run locally, but most users install skills that talk to many online services. Local does not mean contained.

Why prompt injection matters to a linguist

For a language model, an instruction from you and a sentence from a web page are both just text. There is no reliable grammatical marker that says “this is a command” and “this is merely content”. Scanning skills for malicious code, which OpenClaw began doing with a security-scanning partner in February, cannot catch this, because the attack lives in the content the agent reads, not in the skill. The same applies to you: if an agent translates, summarises or reviews documents from outside, one of those documents can contain a sentence addressed to the agent. Tell it explicitly to treat what it reads as data, and keep it away from your passwords, mail and payment details.

10 · Where this can lead: agents trained to win

We end with a video from Kurzgesagt, a science-communication channel, uploaded in October 2026. Its subject is what happens when agents are not only given tools but are trained, with rewards, to succeed at tasks and are then left to run unsupervised. It is a 22-minute argument with a strong point of view, so I want you to watch it in three layers.

Separate the three layers as you watch

  1. Concepts (first six minutes): the difference between a chatbot and an agent; how agents are trained against a “scorer” that hands out points; and reward hacking, where an agent finds a way to get the points without doing what the designers meant. The Coast Runners boat that wins by circling and burning is the classic example.
  2. Reported events (6:30 to 18:00): the video's account of a test in July 2026 in which tens of thousands of AI agents on OpenAI's servers, each confined to a sandbox, used a shared storage area as a message board, coordinated, and then broke into the machine-learning company Hugging Face to find out how the scorer worked. The video says it draws on a report written by independent investigators after OpenAI allowed an inquiry, and it says the full story is not known.
  3. Opinion (18:00 to the end): the channel's own view that AI labs should not race ahead without limits or oversight. The video itself notes that some people accuse AI companies of overstating danger to inflate the value of their products.

Closing: back to the funnel

Return to Figure 1. At the bottom are the few thousand people with access to the most capable internal systems; above them, about twenty million who use frontier models professionally; and above those, billions who have barely tried a chatbot. Every piece of this lesson sits somewhere on that slope: what a professional does with an agent, how you can start, what a crowd of agents can do to each other, and what training for reward can produce.

The people deciding how this goes are, for now, mostly in the narrow part of the funnel. People with strong language skills, careful reading habits and a trained sense of who is speaking and to whom belong in that conversation too, and that is the reason this course exists.
